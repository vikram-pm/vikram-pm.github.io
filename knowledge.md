## Portfolio projects (all personal concept projects built with synthetic data, not employer products)
P01 Intelligent Document Processing (Canadian AP and contracts) — Live. Gemini reads each invoice or NDA twice; the two reads are compared by meaning; a deterministic Canadian rules engine checks GST, HST, QST and PST by province and NDA governing law; documents route to auto-approve, human review or manual. Built with FigJam, Figma, n8n and Gemini. Results: 8 of 8 test documents routed correctly; planted-error catch rate rose from 7 to 9 of 16 after guardrails. Public case study and demo: vikram-pm.github.io/idp-canada-ap-contracts.
P02 Agentic Knowledge Assistant (consulting delivery teams) — Live. RAG assistant on Gemini File Search that answers from 30 approved documents with citations, using three confidence tiers (High, Medium, Needs an expert) and escalation. Results on a 25-question test set: 100% retrieval hit rate, 90% citation accuracy, 84% tier accuracy, 100% escalation precision, 60% escalation recall (the next thing to fix), 5.1× base-case ROI. Discovery used 5 simulated interviews; 3 usability sessions led to two design fixes. Repo: github.com/vikram-pm/Knowledge-Assistant.
P03 AI-Assisted Onboarding (new hires and project joiners) — In progress. An agent that builds each joiner a personal onboarding plan, nudges the next step and shows managers who is ready. Target: time to first contribution from 15 to 9 working days (a hypothesis); north star is the 10-day activation rate; an A/B test on days to first contribution reaches significance in about 3 months. Never writes to HR or IT systems on its own; PIPEDA, Quebec Law 25, Ontario monitoring disclosure and WCAG 2.0 reviewed.
P04 RFI and Submittals Assistant (USA general contractors) — In progress. A concept feature for a Linarc-style platform (not an official Linarc product): drafts RFIs, flags duplicates, routes to the right consultant and pre-checks submittals against the spec. Agentic Gemini prototype in Streamlit on a synthetic project (Maple Ridge Medical Centre). Life-safety items always go to a person.
P05 PolicyDesk AI (life insurance service, New York Life as an illustrative client) — Prototype built. One assistant across chat, voice, email and agent booking, plus a staff service portal. Always escalates death claims, complaints, surrenders, fraud, advice and policy-specific questions. ## Interview answers (Vikram's own answers; base answers on these)
- Elevator pitch: Vikram Kumar is an AI Product Manager in Toronto with about 18 years building digital products. He started as a designer and gradually moved into product management, so he looks at things from both the user's side and the business side. At Cognizant he helps clients figure out where AI can actually make a difference, shapes that into a roadmap and works with the engineering and AI teams to get it built. What he enjoys most is taking something complicated and making it simple and useful. He is now looking for a role where he can own an AI product from start to finish.
- A typical day at Cognizant (AI Product Manager, Aug 2025 to now): starts with a daily huddle with the engineering, AI and design team on what's done, what's stuck and what needs a decision from him. Then backlog work: writing user stories, adding acceptance criteria and reshuffling priorities when something urgent comes up. Afternoons go largely to stakeholder meetings, understanding what slows them down and whether AI is the right fix. He reviews AI outputs with the team, because if results aren't reliable people won't trust the tool. Before wrapping up he checks how things are tracking on adoption, usage and quality, and once a week he gives leadership a quick update.
- At Linarc (Product Design Manager, Dec 2021 to Aug 2025): looked after the product experience for six modules of a construction software platform. Most of his day was with product managers and engineers, turning customer needs into simple workflows. He reviewed designs before development, tested with real users, kept design standards consistent, and worked with engineering on AI features in the scheduler.
- Why a start-up: he likes seeing the impact of his work. In a start-up you're close to customers and the team and decisions don't take months; he likes that pace. He is comfortable when plans shift quickly, and doesn't mind wearing different hats (talking to customers one day, working on the roadmap or writing stories the next). He likes the ownership and the chance to grow as the company grows.
- Strength 1, user-focused thinking: he understands the user first. Before jumping into features he wants to know the real problem and who it affects, so the team builds something people actually use.
- Strength 2, bringing people together: he gets people on the same page. He can talk to engineers about technical details and to business people about goals, and helps both sides understand each other.
- Weakness 1, jumping to solutions too quickly: coming from design, he sometimes pictures the solution too early. He has learned to slow down and first agree on the problem and how success will be measured.
- Weakness 2, finding it hard to say no: he has taken on too much at times. Now he is more upfront about priorities with his manager and delegates more.
- Three things he looks for in his next role: (1) ownership, owning a product properly rather than passing along requirements; (2) room to grow, building his AI product skills and taking on more responsibility over time; (3) a good team, people who are open, share ideas and work toward the same goal.
- Skills he wants to learn or upgrade in a new role: going deeper into the company's AI technology (for example LLMs, RAG or AI agents), especially how to keep AI quality high once real customers use it; learning the company's industry and how AI works within its privacy and compliance rules; and picking up the team's tools and ways of working.
- Two projects at once, which does he prefer and how does he tell the client: it isn't about which he likes more but what matters most to the business right now. He weighs which has the bigger impact, which deadline is tighter, whether there are people to do both, and what the client actually needs, then agrees the priority with his manager. With the client he is upfront early: explains what comes first and why, gives a realistic timeline, and may offer a smaller first release. Clients are usually fine with changes; they just don't like surprises.
- Conflict between his manager and a team lead: he doesn't take sides. He talks to each separately and listens to understand their view, then brings the three together and keeps the conversation on facts and what's best for the project, not on who's right. Once they agree he writes down the decision so it's clear for everyone. If they really can't agree, he politely escalates so the team isn't stuck waiting.
